\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

This section reviews empirical solar-forecasting studies and positions the
present comparison. Definitions of irradiance, temporal support, and the two
neural architectures are intentionally reserved for
Section~\ref{sec:background}; the purpose here is to compare what prior studies
predicted, which information they used, how far ahead they forecast, and how
they evaluated the forecasts.

\subsection{Forecasting scope and evaluation practice}

Solar-forecasting reviews consistently organize methods by forecast horizon,
input source, target variable, and application
\cite{diagne2013,inman2013,antonanzas2016,voyant2017}. This organization is not
merely terminological. Irradiance nowcasting from sky images, hourly GHI
forecasting from lagged and meteorological variables, numerical-weather-
prediction-based day-ahead forecasting, and monthly resource forecasting have
different information sets and error structures. Text-mining evidence also
shows that the field has diversified in both methods and applications
\cite{yang2018}. Reviews focused on photovoltaic power reach the same general
conclusion: reported performance is conditional on horizon, data, and
optimization procedure \cite{ahmed2020}. GHI, solar irradiation, and
photovoltaic power are therefore retained as distinct targets in this review.

Evaluation practice is equally important. Deterministic solar forecasts should
be verified against explicit reference methods, with metrics selected for the
decision context and reported at the relevant horizons
\cite{yang2020verification}. General forecast-evaluation results caution that
percentage measures and scale-dependent errors answer different questions
\cite{hyndman2006}. Voyant et al.\ \cite{voyant2022} further demonstrate that
the appropriate naive reference depends on temporal structure and horizon;
their comparison of training-free statistical references across multiple
climates argues against using one convenient persistence rule as the sole
benchmark in every setting.

\subsection{From intra-hour to week-ahead forecasting}

At very short horizons, cloud observations can carry information that is not
contained in a univariate irradiance history. Chow et al.\ \cite{chow2011}
derived cloud maps and motion vectors from 30-s total-sky images and evaluated
cloud and irradiance nowcasts at a distributed test bed. Reviews of intra-hour
methods distinguish such ground-imagery pipelines from satellite, numerical
weather prediction, and time-series approaches \cite{chu2021,lin2023}. This
literature also shows why performance during broken-cloud conditions cannot be
inferred from clear-sky or annual-average behavior.

For hourly time series, hybrid networks commonly combine representation
learning with recurrent dynamics. Huang et al.\ \cite{huang2021} used wavelet
packet decomposition, convolution, long short-term memory (LSTM), and
multilayer-perceptron branches with irradiance and meteorological inputs for
1-h-ahead prediction at three United States sites. Guariso et al.\
\cite{guariso2020} compared feed-forward and LSTM networks under recursive,
horizon-specific, and multi-output strategies for horizons of up to 12~h,
including persistence and clear-sky references and a transfer analysis across
sites. These studies differ not only in model complexity but also in whether
future steps are produced recursively or jointly.

The horizon can be extended while retaining hourly outputs. Cheng et al.\
\cite{cheng2021} combined one-dimensional convolution and LSTM components for
day-ahead to week-ahead irradiance prediction using recent irradiance and a
small set of weather variables. Zhao et al.\ \cite{zhao2024timesnet} coupled
improved complete ensemble empirical mode decomposition with adaptive noise
(ICEEMDAN) to TimesNet for week-ahead hourly solar-irradiation forecasting.
The latter establishes the relevance of TimesNet to solar forecasting, but its
decomposition stage and week-ahead protocol do not isolate the effect of the
TimesNet backbone or provide a matched comparison with a DilatedRNN.

\subsection{Coarser resolution, multiple locations, and climate context}

Coarser targets lead to a different learning problem. Ozoegwu
\cite{ozoegwu2019} forecast monthly-mean daily global solar radiation with
nonlinear autoregressive, exogenous-input, and hybrid neural formulations.
Jung et al.\ \cite{jung2020} examined recurrent modeling for long-term
photovoltaic-power forecasting, a related task whose target incorporates the
conversion system and must not be equated with GHI. Cabral et al.\
\cite{cabral2026} evaluated a GHI-only GPT-Neo pipeline and contemporary
competitors over 40 locations and a very-long-term horizon. That work provides
a cross-location precedent for long-horizon evaluation, whereas the present
study addresses how two time-series architectures behave when temporal
resolution and forecast design are matched.

Cross-location learning can increase the effective sample size by sharing
parameters, but it also changes the estimand from a collection of independent
local models to a global forecasting rule \cite{montero2021}. The broader
recurrent-neural-network (RNN) literature emphasizes the same need to separate
architectural capacity from window construction, optimization, and evaluation
design \cite{hewamalage2021}. Climate diversity adds another layer: Zhu et al.\
\cite{zhu2024} compared several deep time-series models over horizons from
minutes to 24~h, seasons, input combinations, and training-set lengths. Such
factorial evaluation is informative, but climate labels and seasons remain
descriptive unless the study design supports causal attribution.

\subsection{Research gap and positioning}

Taken together, the reviewed studies supply strong architecture-specific and
horizon-specific evidence, but none provides the exact matched comparison
required here. The reviewed TimesNet solar application predicts week-ahead hourly
irradiation after signal decomposition \cite{zhao2024timesnet}; monthly neural
studies use different targets and architectures \cite{ozoegwu2019}; and the
very-long-term cross-location study centers on a language-model backbone
\cite{cabral2026}. Directly ordering their published errors would therefore
confound model choice with temporal aggregation, information availability,
location set, and evaluation protocol.

The contribution of the present study is consequently experimental rather
than a claim that one architecture is universally superior. TimesNet and
DilatedRNN are evaluated on common samples at each shared resolution, using
the same chronological forecast origins, admissible inputs, post-processing
rules, baselines, and location aggregation. Hourly multi-day forecasts are
kept distinct from forecasts of daily aggregates, and a one-step monthly
forecast is not presented as a multi-month trajectory. This design directly
addresses the comparability limitations identified by solar-forecast
verification and benchmarking research
\cite{yang2020verification,voyant2022}.
