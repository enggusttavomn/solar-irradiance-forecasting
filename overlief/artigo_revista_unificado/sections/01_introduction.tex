\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Pathways consistent with deep emissions reductions combine a rapid expansion
of low-emission electricity with the electrification of end uses
\cite{ipcc2022energy,irena2024weto}. Declining renewable-energy costs can
reinforce this coupling \cite{luderer2022}, while scenario-based evidence
indicates that the diffusion of solar photovoltaics may acquire substantial
self-sustaining momentum before mid-century \cite{nijsse2023}. These
system-level trends do not remove the operational difficulty created by the
weather-dependent and diurnal nature of the solar resource. They instead make
the temporal value of solar information increasingly important for planning,
scheduling, and energy management.

The industrial sector is a particularly demanding setting for this transition.
Its heterogeneous processes require combinations of efficiency, material
changes, low-carbon fuels, electrification, and low-emission electricity
\cite{ipcc2022industry,rissman2020}. Direct electrification can reduce
emissions from technically suitable industrial heat uses, but its potential
depends on process temperature, infrastructure, and the carbon intensity of
the electricity supply \cite{madeddu2020}. On-site and procured renewable
electricity are therefore relevant options for manufacturing, rather than a
universal substitute for all industrial energy inputs
\cite{irena2014manufacturing,padmanathan2018}. Adoption also faces
site-specific financial, regulatory, technical, and organizational barriers
\cite{dhingra2023}.

Regulatory drivers must likewise be stated with their jurisdiction and
conditions. For example, Directive (EU) 2024/1275 establishes a phased
framework for solar-energy installations on specified building categories
when technically, economically, and functionally suitable
\cite{eu2024epbd}. It does not apply to the Brazilian, Mexican, or United
States locations examined here. Public corporate disclosures provide
illustrative evidence of manufacturing-related renewable-energy adoption:
Nestl\'e reports that its KitKat production lines in Ca\c{c}apava use
predominantly solar electricity \cite{nestle2024biometano}; Mercedes-Benz
describes rooftop photovoltaics and storage at Factory~56
\cite{mercedesfactory56}; and Tesla and Ford report solar installations at
selected manufacturing sites \cite{tesla2021impact,ford2023valencia}. These
examples motivate the industrial context, but they are not evidence that
photovoltaic systems are installed at any of the ten factory-associated
coordinates used in this study.

Solar forecasting spans distinct physical and operational regimes. Intra-hour
forecasts can track cloud motion, hourly and day-ahead forecasts support
short-term scheduling, and forecasts aggregated over days or months address
progressively longer planning scales \cite{diagne2013,inman2013}. The terms
short-term and long-term are consequently incomplete unless temporal
resolution and forecast horizon are both reported
\cite{antonanzas2016,yang2018}. A model that is effective for a direct
72-hour sequence of hourly global horizontal irradiance (GHI) is not thereby
established as effective for daily means or a one-step monthly target.
Conversely, low error on an
aggregated monthly series cannot be compared numerically with error on an
hourly series without accounting for the different targets, sample sizes, and
variability.

This article studies GHI at ten geographic points associated with
electric-vehicle manufacturing facilities. The points span different
latitudes and atmospheric regimes and are used as a geographically meaningful
test bed. The irradiance records are satellite-derived, model-based estimates
from the National Solar Radiation Database (NSRDB) \cite{sengupta2018}; they
are not measurements from factory sensors.
No factory production, electricity consumption, photovoltaic capacity, or
other proprietary operational data enter the analysis. Accordingly, the
forecasts characterize the solar resource at the selected grid cells and are
not forecasts of factory load or photovoltaic power output.

Cabral et al.\ \cite{cabral2026} investigated very-long-term GHI forecasting
across 40 climatically diverse locations using a GHI-only GPT-Neo pipeline and
a broad set of competitors. The present article addresses a different
question and does not reuse those numerical results. It consolidates and
extends two protocol-specific investigations developed by the present authors:
a compact TimesNet for direct hourly forecasting and a Dilated Recurrent
Neural Network (DilatedRNN) for one-step monthly forecasting. Those preliminary
protocols differed in resolution, context, horizon, origins, competitors, and
primary metrics. Their reported errors are retained only as development
lineage; the architecture comparison in this article is rebuilt on matched
samples within every task.

The research question is therefore: \emph{how should TimesNet and DilatedRNN
be compared when the available information, temporal resolution, forecast
horizon, chronological origins, and evaluation rules are controlled across
locations?} The article makes four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item it defines a common multi-resolution formulation that distinguishes
  hourly multi-step, genuinely daily, and one-step monthly forecasting;
  \item it specifies matched samples, chronological partitions, forecast
  origins, direct outputs, post-processing, and transparent baselines for the
  two architectures at every shared task;
  \item it reports the complete benchmark before the focused architecture
  comparison, retaining macro, location, horizon, and seed-level evidence so
  that average performance cannot conceal local losses or strong simple
  references; and
  \item it selects favorable, unfavorable, geographic, and meteorological
  cases by auditable rules, without treating ten locations as evidence of a
  causal climate effect or inferring rain from low GHI alone.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the article is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related_work} critically reviews the forecasting literature
and identifies the comparison gap. Section~\ref{sec:background} defines GHI,
temporal aggregation, forecasting horizons, TimesNet, and DilatedRNN.
Subsequent sections document the data and feature engineering, the common
methodology, the experimental design, and the evaluation metrics before
presenting the results, limitations, and conclusions.
